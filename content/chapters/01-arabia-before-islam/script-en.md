# Arabia Before Islam

## s01 -- The Land
Image: desert/dunes-day.svg | pan-right | Empty desert dunes under a pale sky. No figures.
Audio: Voice only.

We begin with Arabia, the setting of this life. <!-- n -->

Arabia is a vast peninsula, bounded by sea on three sides.
The Red Sea lies to the west, the Arabian Sea to the south, and the Gulf to the east.
Much of its interior is desert and dry steppe.
Rain is scarce, there are no great rivers, and life gathers around wells and oases. <!-- c01-001 -->

In the sixth century, most of its people lived in tribes.
Some were settled in towns.
Many were nomads, moving with their herds in search of pasture. <!-- c01-002 -->

## s02 -- Between Empires
Image: maps/arabia.svg | static | A map of the Arabian peninsula between the Byzantine and Persian empires, with Makkah, Yathrib, Najran and Sana'a marked. | 0 | 24% 50%
Audio: Voice only.

To the north and west lay the Byzantine Empire, which ruled Syria and Egypt.
To the north and east lay the Persian Empire of the Sasanians.
The two powers were rivals, and they fought one another again and again. <!-- c01-003 -->

The Qur'an itself refers to this struggle, in the opening verses of the chapter named The Romans. <!-- c01-004 -->

Neither empire ruled the heart of Arabia.
The region of the Hijaz, in the west of the peninsula, belonged to no king. <!-- c01-005 -->

There was no state, no common law, and no single authority.
A person's protection came from his tribe. <!-- c01-002 -->

## s03 -- Makkah and the House
Image: makkah/valley-dawn.svg | slow-zoom | First light over a valley town ringed by mountains. No figures.
Audio: Voice only.

In the Hijaz, in a dry valley surrounded by mountains, stood the town of Makkah. <!-- c01-005 -->

The Qur'an records the prayer of the Prophet Ibrahim, peace be upon him, when he settled some of his family there.
Its meaning is:
"Our Lord, I have settled some of my offspring in a valley without cultivation, near Your Sacred House." <!-- c01-006 -->

The Qur'an also tells us that Ibrahim and his son Ismail raised the foundations of that House, <!-- c01-007 -->
and that it was the first House established for mankind. <!-- c01-008 -->

That House is the Ka'bah.
Because of it, Makkah was a place of pilgrimage, and people travelled to it from across Arabia. <!-- c01-009 -->

By the sixth century, the tribe of Quraysh held Makkah and were the custodians of the Ka'bah. <!-- c01-010 -->
Makkah had no farmland, so Quraysh lived by trade.
The Qur'an mentions their two caravan journeys: one in winter, and one in summer. <!-- c01-011 -->

## s04 -- How the Worship Changed
Image: makkah/valley-night.svg | pan-left | A valley town at night beneath the stars. No figures.
Audio: Voice only.

Ibrahim had called people to worship Allah alone. <!-- c01-012 -->
But over the generations, the people of Arabia turned away from that. <!-- c01-013 -->

The Messenger of Allah ﷺ spoke of a man named Amr ibn Luhayy, of the tribe of Khuza'ah,
as the first to introduce one of the idolatrous customs of the Arabs. <!-- c01-013 -->
The historians of the Seerah report that it was he who first brought idols to Makkah. <!-- c01-014 -->

In time, idols were set up around the Ka'bah itself.
On the day Makkah was opened to the Muslims, many years later, there were three hundred and sixty of them around the House. <!-- c01-015 -->

The Qur'an names several of the idols the Arabs worshipped, among them al-Lat, al-Uzza, and Manat. <!-- c01-016 -->

Yet many of the Arabs still acknowledged Allah as the Creator.
The Qur'an says that if they were asked who created the heavens and the earth, they would answer: "Allah." <!-- c01-017 -->
But they directed their worship to others beside Him, saying that these would bring them nearer to Allah. <!-- c01-018 -->

## s05 -- The Society
Image: desert/oasis-dusk.svg | slow-zoom | Palm trees at an oasis at dusk. No figures.
Audio: Voice only.

It was a society of sharp contrasts. <!-- n -->

The Arabs of that time prized certain qualities.
Generosity to the guest. Courage. Loyalty to one's word.
And a deep love of eloquent speech.
Their poetry was their record, and their pride. <!-- c01-019 -->

But there was also great injustice.
Tribes raided one another, and feuds could last for generations.
The weak, the orphan, and the stranger without a tribe had little protection. <!-- c01-020 -->
Wine and gambling were widespread. <!-- c01-021 -->

And the Qur'an speaks of the man who, when given news of a daughter, would darken with grief,
and of the infant girl who was buried alive. <!-- c01-022 -->

## s06 -- Those Who Searched
Image: mountains/ridges-dusk.svg | pan-left | Layered mountain ridges at dusk. No figures.
Audio: Voice only.

Not everyone accepted the worship of idols. <!-- c01-024 -->

There were Jewish communities in Arabia, among them the tribes of Yathrib.
And there were Christian communities, among them the people of Najran. <!-- c01-023 -->

There were also a few individuals among the Arabs who turned away from the idols on their own,
and sought the religion of Ibrahim. <!-- c01-024 -->

One of them was Zayd ibn Amr ibn Nufayl.
It is narrated that he would not eat what had been slaughtered in the name of idols. <!-- c01-025 -->

Asma, the daughter of Abu Bakr, said that she saw him standing with his back against the Ka'bah,
telling Quraysh that none of them was upon the religion of Ibrahim but him.
He would also save infant girls from being buried, and take them into his own care. <!-- c01-026 -->

The historians report that Zayd died a few years before the revelation began. <!-- c01-027 -->

## s07 -- The Eve
Image: desert/dunes-night.svg | slow-zoom | Desert dunes at night under a thin moon. No figures.
Audio: Voice only.

This, then, was Arabia in the sixth century.
A land without a king.
A sacred House surrounded by idols.
A people with noble qualities, and deep wrongs. <!-- c01-002 c01-009 c01-015 c01-019 c01-020 -->
